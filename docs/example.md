# A short fictional example

[简体中文](example.zh-CN.md) · [Homepage](../README.md)

This example is invented to explain the workflow. It is not a real evaluation or a promise of better competition results.

## Project and story

A team has built a prototype for logging laboratory equipment handovers. It records a scan and confirmation during borrowing and return. In one internal trial, ten people completed the assigned workflow. No time savings or long-term reduction in missing records have been measured.

The story: records can lag behind equipment handovers; the prototype puts logging into the action itself; the first trial checks workflow completion; further work must test sustained use.

## A reasoned slide move

Current order: **problem → trial results → workflow → next steps**.

Suggestion: move the workflow page before the trial results. The audience can then understand what the participants were asked to do before assessing the result. No new evidence is created by moving the slide.

The user may accept this move or keep the original order. The final script always follows the actual chosen PPT, rather than assigning imaginary page numbers to a proposed deck.

## Connected speech

**Workflow page:** “We put the record into the handover step, so it can be captured when equipment changes hands.”

**Trial page:** “We then checked whether people could complete that workflow. Ten testers completed the assigned steps; we have not yet measured time savings or long-term use.”

The second page builds on the action explained by the first. It also keeps the trial's conclusion within the evidence.

## A likely panel question

**Question:** “What do ten completed trials actually prove?”

**Answer:** “They show that these ten testers completed the assigned workflow in this trial. We did not measure time or long-term use, so we cannot yet say that it improves efficiency or reduces missing records.”

## A tougher question

**Confirmed contribution:** the presenter designed the interface and handover workflow; a teammate implemented storage; the supervisor participated in requirements review.

**Question:** “Did you actually make this yourself?”

**Answer:** “It was a team effort. I designed the interface and handover workflow; my teammate implemented storage, and our supervisor reviewed the requirements. I can explain the design decisions in the part I worked on.”

If those contributions are unknown, the skill asks instead of filling them in. This is the same principle used in the separate tough-question rehearsal document.
